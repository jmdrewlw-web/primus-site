# What Happens When You Rush the Construction Process

There is a particular kind of pressure that builds in the months before a planned opening. You've signed a lease. You've told your staff. You've started telling patients or clients. And now your contractor is telling you something you don't want to hear — that the timeline you had in your head isn't the timeline reality is willing to cooperate with. The natural response, especially for operators who have spent their careers moving fast and making things happen, is to push. Compress the schedule. Skip the slow parts. Just get it done.

This is one of the most expensive decisions you can make. Not because the project won't finish — it usually does — but because the building you rushed into is the building you're going to operate out of for the next decade. And rushed buildings have a way of reminding you, every single day, that corners were cut.

The damage starts before a single wall goes up. The programming phase — the period where an experienced design-build team systematically works through your actual operational needs — is the part that gets sacrificed first when schedules get compressed. Programming isn't glamorous. It's a series of conversations about how many exam rooms you actually need, how patients or clients will move through the space, where staff will prepare and recover and store equipment, what your technology infrastructure needs to support. Operators who skip this phase often don't realize what they've lost until they're six months into operations and the layout is fighting them every day. The dental practice that programmed four operatories instead of six because nobody ran the math on patient volume. The veterinary clinic whose treatment area is positioned so that every animal has to pass through the waiting room on the way in and out. The daycare facility that built six classrooms when licensing ratios required eight. These aren't design failures. They're programming failures, and they happen when pressure is applied before the thinking is done.

The second casualty of a compressed schedule is design quality, and this one has immediate financial consequences that show up on your invoice, not just in your operational experience. Design is where problems get solved on paper, cheaply. When design gets rushed — drawings that should take ten weeks get pushed through in four — the decisions that didn't get resolved on paper get resolved in the field. And resolving things in the field means change orders. A structural beam that lands exactly where the plumbing was supposed to run. An HVAC trunk that conflicts with the ceiling height in the reception area. An electrical panel that ends up in the wrong room because nobody flagged it during design review. Each of these is a solvable problem, but solving them mid-construction costs three to five times what it would have cost to prevent them in design. Operators who pressure design timelines often end up paying more in change orders than they would have paid just to do design properly the first time.

Then there is what happens to your subcontractors when they're handed a compressed schedule. The best subcontractors — the electricians and plumbers and HVAC mechanics who actually know what they're doing — are busy. They have backlogs. When a general contractor commits to an accelerated schedule, they sometimes have to backfill with whoever is available. And when the schedule is tight and the subcontractors are overextended, things get missed. Inspections get called before work is ready. Work that should have been done one way gets done a faster way. Nobody on a job site sets out to do bad work, but bad work happens when people are moving too fast with too little time to think. The plumbing that wasn't roughed in quite right. The electrical panel that doesn't have the capacity expansion slots you'll need in three years. The HVAC system that wasn't balanced properly at startup and runs loud and uneven forever after. Small things. Things that each feel manageable on their own. But they accumulate.

Dr. Gleason at Gleason Dental in Nebraska closed on a Thursday and opened on a Monday after a build that was executed properly — not because corners were cut, but because the planning was done right so execution could move cleanly. That kind of turnaround is possible when programming and design are complete before the first nail is driven. It is not possible when you try to compress the planning to speed up the construction.

The operators who have built multiple facilities understand something that first-time builders often don't: the construction period is actually the cheapest time to get things right. Once the walls are up and the building is occupied and you're trying to run a practice or a clinic or a facility out of it, every problem costs dramatically more to fix. The door that swings the wrong way. The room that's three feet too small. The server room that isn't climate-controlled. These are not problems you adapt to. They are problems you either live with for years or pay a significant premium to correct.

The pressure to rush is real, and it comes from legitimate places — lease deadlines, staff expectations, competitive pressure, financing timelines. A construction partner who understands your business will help you find the actual available speed without sacrificing the things that cannot be recovered. There is a real schedule, and there is a fantasy schedule. Building to the real schedule is how you get a building that works. Building to the fantasy schedule is how you get a building that you're still apologizing for years later.
