# The Contractor Started Building Before the Drawings Were Done

There is a version of this story where it ends fine. The contractor gets started, details get worked out on the fly, and the project comes in close enough to budget and schedule that nobody talks about it. That version exists. It is not common.

The more common version starts the same way: an owner who has been waiting months for permits, watching competitors open new locations, fielding questions from staff about when the new space will be ready. The contractor says he can get crew on-site next week if they just start framing — the drawings are 80% there, and they can work out the details as they go. The owner says yes. The framing crew shows up Monday.

What the owner did not know is that "80% there" in this context meant schematic design drawings — essentially a floor plan and some elevations showing what the architect wants the building to look like. What was missing was everything that tells a crew how to actually build it.

Construction documents are not just a floor plan. A complete set includes architectural drawings with exact partition locations, door and hardware schedules, ceiling heights at every point in the building, and finish specifications. It includes structural drawings showing the framing system, load paths, and connection details — the information the framing crew actually needs before they pick up a nail gun. It includes mechanical drawings showing every HVAC unit, duct route, and diffuser location. Electrical drawings with panel schedules, circuiting, device locations, and equipment connections. Plumbing drawings with drain locations, supply lines, and fixture rough-in specifications. These drawings do not exist in isolation — they are coordinated. The structural engineer has seen the mechanical layout and confirmed there is clearance for ductwork. The electrician knows where the equipment lands so rough-in happens in the right place the first time.

When you start building before this coordination is complete, every trade is working from incomplete information. The framing crew builds walls in locations that made sense on the schematic plan. Then the mechanical engineer finishes the duct layout and discovers that one of those walls is exactly where the main supply trunk needs to run. Someone has to make a decision: move the wall or move the duct. Moving the wall means the framing crew tears out what they built and starts over. Moving the duct changes the ceiling height in the next room, which affects the millwork specification, which the owner now needs to re-approve. None of this was in the original plan. All of it costs money.

This is not a hypothetical. It happens on almost every project where construction starts ahead of complete documents. The specific problems vary — it might be equipment utility rough-ins that end up in the wrong location because the equipment vendor's submittals weren't reviewed before the slab was poured, or plumbing stacks that conflict with structural beams that weren't on the schematic drawings, or electrical panel location that made sense until the equipment layout was finalized. The mechanism is always the same: a decision gets made under time pressure using incomplete information, and then reality arrives and demands a different decision.

The cost of changes during construction is not linear. A partition location that costs nothing to shift on paper costs a day of labor and materials to move once it is framed. It costs more if drywall is already hung. It costs significantly more if electrical rough-in is already in. Every discipline that has touched the work you are now undoing adds to the change order. And because the changes were driven by scope that was never fully defined in the original documents, they are legitimate. The contractor is not padding the bill. The work genuinely was not in the contract.

This is the part that surprises owners who signed what they thought was a fixed-price contract. A fixed price is only fixed for the scope it covers. When scope is undefined — because the drawings were incomplete when construction started — the contractor has a valid basis to charge for work outside the original scope. The fixed-price contract becomes a cost-plus contract through the back door of the change order log.

The math usually works against "just get started." A project that starts on schematic drawings might break ground two weeks earlier. Those two weeks typically get consumed in the first month by the delays caused by incomplete information — crew standing around waiting for decisions, work being done and undone, inspectors flagging items that the documents did not adequately address. The net time savings approaches zero. The cost savings are negative. The stress is higher throughout because decisions that should have been made calmly in a design meeting are now being made under construction pressure with real money on the line.

There is a reason a complete set of construction documents takes time to produce. It is not bureaucratic delay. It is the process of making every decision — every room size, every outlet location, every equipment clearance, every structural connection — before any of those decisions cost money to change. The disciplines need to coordinate. The owner needs to review. The numbers need to be checked against the budget. When that work is done before construction starts, the crew has everything they need to build without stopping. That is how you get a fast project. Not by starting before you are ready.
