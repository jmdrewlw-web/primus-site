# How to Be the Client Your Contractor Actually Wants to Work With

Every article about construction projects tells operators how to manage a bad contractor. How to protect yourself from change orders, how to read a contract, how to escalate when the job is falling behind. That information has its place. But there is a version of the story that almost never gets told: the role the owner plays in whether a project goes well.

Contractors are not the only variable in a construction project. Owners are a variable too. The owners who get the best outcomes — the ones who come in on budget, finish on schedule, and end up with the space they envisioned — are the ones who show up ready to be a good partner. That is not a soft statement about attitude. It is a practical description of specific behaviors that either accelerate or delay a project.

Here are the specific behaviors from the owner side that create problems.

Changing scope mid-project without understanding the impact. This is the most common source of cost overruns and schedule delays in commercial construction, and it almost always originates with the owner. "Can we move that wall six inches?" "What if we swapped the tile for this other tile we just found?" "We want to add a utility sink in the breakroom." Each of these changes sounds small. Each of them has cost implications, schedule implications, and potentially permit implications. The contractor cannot absorb scope changes for free. When owners change scope and are surprised by the resulting change order, the surprise usually reflects an unrealistic assumption about what changes cost, not contractor opportunism.

Making decisions slowly when the contractor is waiting. Every material on a project has a lead time. If tile has a six-week lead time and you tell the contractor you will decide on tile next week, you have potentially delayed your project by five weeks when all the lead times stack up. Contractors build project schedules around owner decision milestones. When owners miss those milestones, the job site waits — and waiting job sites are expensive. The question to ask at the beginning of every project is: what decisions do I need to make, and when does the contractor need them by? Then honor those dates.

Communicating through intermediaries who lack decision authority. This one compounds slowly and then suddenly. An owner routes all communication through an office manager or spouse or business partner who does not have authority to approve changes or commit to decisions. The contractor submits a question or a request for direction. The intermediary relays it. The owner responds two days later with follow-up questions. The intermediary relays again. By the time a decision is made, a week has passed. Multiply this pattern across a hundred decisions in a 16-week build and the schedule impact is real. Identify a single point of contact on the owner side who has authority to make decisions, and have that person communicate directly with the project manager.

Showing up on the job site unannounced and talking to workers directly. This one damages projects in ways that are not always visible. Workers on a job site follow direction from the site superintendent and project manager. When an owner walks the site and has casual conversations with workers — even friendly, well-intentioned conversations — they create confusion about scope, authority, and direction. A worker who hears the owner mention they want something done a certain way does not always know whether that is an approved change or an offhand comment. The right channel for any question, concern, or request is through the project manager. Walk the site on scheduled visits. Keep the conversation with the PM.

What good client behavior looks like in practice is not complicated. Make your decisions on the timeline the contractor gives you — put the decision milestones in your calendar and treat them as real deadlines. Communicate through the designated channel. Bring your questions to the project manager, not to the workers. If you want to change something mid-project, ask the contractor to tell you the cost and schedule impact before approving it — and then actually evaluate whether it is worth the impact. Understand that your responsiveness is part of the project timeline, not a variable outside of it.

The best projects are genuine partnerships. The contractor brings expertise in construction, logistics, and coordination. The owner brings knowledge of their business, their operational needs, and their patient or customer experience goals. Those two sets of knowledge need to combine — and they combine best when the communication is clear, the decisions come on time, and both parties are working toward the same outcome rather than each trying to protect against the other.

Operators who approach a construction project with a partner mindset consistently get better results than operators who approach it with a purely adversarial posture. The adversarial posture is sometimes appropriate — there are bad contractors, and protecting yourself from them is legitimate. But starting from suspicion and defensiveness creates a dynamic that costs everyone, including the owner.

The single most useful thing an owner can do going into a construction project is get clear on what they need, stay in communication through the right channels, make decisions on time, and trust the team they hired. If the team is not trustworthy, that is a different problem — and the solution is a better team, not a more adversarial stance with the wrong one.
