# The Contractor Stopped Returning Calls. Here's What Comes Next.

It starts with something small. The crew that was on-site every day does not show up Monday. The superintendent does not return the call by noon, which is unusual, but you give it until end of day. By Wednesday you have left three messages and sent two emails. Thursday, a subcontractor you have never spoken to before calls your cell phone asking when he is going to get paid — apparently there is $22,000 owed for rough electrical work completed three weeks ago. Friday, the job site is quiet and nothing has moved.

Your contractor has gone dark.

This is not a rare event. It is common enough that there is a recognized pattern to how it unfolds, a predictable sequence of causes, and a realistic set of options for owners who find themselves mid-project with an unresponsive GC. None of the options are good. The question is which response limits the damage.

**How contractors get into this position**

Most contractor abandonment — whether it is the sudden informal kind or the slower formal kind that ends in bankruptcy — starts with a cash flow problem that compounds over several months. A contractor takes on more work than their operating capital can support. Early projects fund later ones. Then something goes wrong on one of the jobs: unexpected site conditions, a cost overrun, a payment dispute with a subcontractor that requires legal fees. Money that was supposed to flow through to your project gets redirected. The crew gets pulled. The superintendent who should be managing your site is now managing the emergency on the other one.

Contractors who overbid to generate volume are particularly vulnerable to this pattern. They win contracts by being the lowest price, which means their margins are thin to begin with. One surprise on one project puts the entire operation under stress.

**Warning signs that appear before abandonment**

The disappearance rarely happens without warning, though the warnings are easy to rationalize in the moment. Crew that was reliably present starts showing up intermittently, with vague explanations about other commitments. Progress slows without a corresponding change in weather or documented delays. The contractor becomes harder to reach — calls returned later, emails answered with less detail. Subcontractors start contacting you directly with questions that should be going through the GC. A sub who worked the site six weeks ago mentions, casually, that he has not been fully paid for that scope.

These are not coincidences. They are the early symptoms of a contractor who is managing a cash flow problem and has not yet told you about it.

**What you can do when you see the signs — or when abandonment happens**

If you are still in the warning-sign phase, there are protective measures. Joint check agreements allow you to co-sign checks to subcontractors alongside the general contractor, which prevents the GC from withholding payment to subs while collecting from you. These are more common in commercial construction than most owners realize, and a financially stable GC should not object to them on a project where they are current on all sub payments.

Familiarize yourself with your state's construction lien laws. Subcontractors who are not paid by the GC can in many states file a lien on your property — even though you paid the GC and have no direct relationship with that sub. Understanding lien deadlines and notice requirements tells you how much time you have to respond to a payment dispute before it becomes a lien on the property.

When abandonment is complete, your first practical task is getting a realistic assessment of where the project stands. Hire an independent contractor or construction manager to walk the site and document the percentage of completion in every scope area. This is not a theoretical exercise — it is the factual basis for every financial decision and legal claim you will make going forward. What is actually done, what is done incorrectly and will need rework, and what has not been started.

**The cost of getting someone else to finish**

The realistic cost of contractor failure and replacement is usually 20 to 40 percent above the original contract value. This is not because replacement contractors are opportunistic. It is because the work is genuinely harder to finish than to start: a new contractor inherits someone else's framing, someone else's rough-in, someone else's decisions. Discovering what was built incorrectly, coordinating with original subcontractors or hiring new ones, and managing the administrative complexity of a project with a broken chain of documentation all costs real money. The original contract price assumed none of this.

If your contractor was bonded — which many are not, particularly on smaller commercial projects — the surety bond exists to pay for exactly this scenario: completing the project when the original contractor cannot or will not. The claims process is not fast and is rarely simple, but a performance bond provides a real financial backstop. Before signing any construction contract, asking for a copy of the contractor's bond and verifying it covers your project value is not an excessive precaution.

**The question worth asking before you sign**

The single most protective thing an owner can do against contractor abandonment is verify the contractor's financial stability before the contract is signed. This means more than looking at a license number. Request financial references from past clients and actually call them. Ask about payment history with subcontractors — call a couple of subs who have worked with this GC and ask whether they were paid on time and in full. Ask the contractor directly what their current backlog looks like and how many projects they are running simultaneously. A contractor at the edge of their capacity, winning new work to fund existing obligations, is a contractor whose failure risk is elevated.

None of this is foolproof. Contractors who have been reliable for years can run into problems on a single bad project. But the due diligence at the start of a relationship is the best lever you have. Once you are mid-project and the crew has stopped showing up, your options are limited and expensive.
