# The Number That Stops Everyone (And Why It Shouldn't Stop You)

There is a moment in almost every construction conversation where the operator gets quiet. It happens when the real cost estimate comes in — not the rough number they'd been carrying in their head, but the actual number based on what they actually need. For a dental practice that's been telling itself "maybe $800K," the number might be $1.4 million. For a veterinary clinic that assumed they could get away with $600K, it might be $1.1 million. For a daycare that figured they'd spend $500K to fix up an old commercial space, it might be $850K after the actual scope becomes clear.

The room gets quiet. The operator nods slowly. You can see the math changing in their head.

This is sticker shock, and it derails more good projects than any other single factor. Not because the number is wrong — usually it's right — but because the operator is comparing it to the wrong thing.

The natural comparison is to what they expected. That gap — between the number they had in their head and the number in front of them — feels like a problem. It isn't. The number in their head was based on incomplete information. The number in front of them is based on what their building actually requires. The gap isn't a cost overrun. It's the cost of getting accurate.

The right comparison is to the revenue the building will generate. A dental practice borrowing $1.4 million at 6.5 percent interest is carrying roughly $9,500 per month in debt service on that loan. If the new facility allows the practice to see 40 more patients per month — a conservative projection for a capacity-constrained practice moving into a larger, purpose-built space — and those patients generate an average of $400 each in production, that's $16,000 per month in new revenue against $9,500 in new debt service. The building pays for itself plus generates positive cash flow from year one.

That math doesn't make the number small. It makes the number meaningful. There's a difference.

The operators who get stuck at sticker shock are the ones who see the cost but not the return. They hold on to the gap between their expectation and reality and treat it as a failure of something — the estimate, the project, the idea. What it actually reflects is the gap between casual thinking and serious planning. Casual thinking assumes construction is cheap. Serious planning accounts for what it actually costs to build a functional, code-compliant, equipment-ready clinical facility in your market.

The operators who move forward are the ones who shift the question. Instead of "how do I get the number down?" they ask "does this building generate enough revenue to support this number?" When the answer to that question is yes — and for most capacity-constrained practices, it is — the path forward becomes clear even if the number is larger than expected.

There is also a version of this conversation that goes differently: the operator gets the number, goes quiet, and says "I had no idea it would be this much, but now that I understand why, I need to rethink my timeline and my financing." That's a good outcome. That's the early conversation doing its job — surfacing the real number before the operator has committed to a site or a loan or a lease that was sized around the wrong assumption.

The number is going to be what it is. The question is whether you find that out before you're committed or after.
