# The Architect Blamed the Contractor. The Contractor Blamed the Architect. You Paid for Both.

It's a story that gets told across every specialty vertical. The project runs long. The budget bloats. The finished space has problems nobody will take responsibility for. You ask the architect why the sterilization room is in the wrong location and he says the contractor deviated from the plans. You ask the contractor and he says the plans were unbuildable as drawn. Meanwhile, you're the one who wrote the checks.

This is not a rare edge case. This is the default outcome when you hire an architect and a contractor separately, put them in motion in parallel — or more accurately, in separate orbits — and expect them to produce something coherent.

## How the Blame Game Gets Started

Architecture and construction are, in theory, sequential. You design first, then you build. In practice, they're deeply interdependent — which means problems that originate in the design phase don't surface until construction, and by then they're expensive to fix and genuinely ambiguous about who caused them.

An architect draws a plan that is technically compliant with code but doesn't fully account for how a dental vacuum system gets run, or where equipment curb-outs would land, or how electrical panel placement affects the back-hall layout. The contractor gets the plans, starts building, hits the problem, improvises, and moves on. Later you notice the vacuum line is running an inexplicable path, or a cabinet run is blocked by an electrical intrusion that wasn't on the plan. Who do you call?

Both of them will have a reasonable-sounding explanation. Neither explanation will help you, because the damage is already built into your walls.

The same pattern plays out in a veterinary clinic — the architect draws a surgical suite without fully accounting for the negative-pressure ventilation requirements, and the HVAC contractor specs to what's on the mechanical drawings. Nobody owns the gap. The problem surfaces at inspection, or when the smell tells you something is wrong on opening day.

In a medical office build, the MEP coordination between the architect's plans and the contractor's execution is where things go wrong most often. Exam room rough-in for oxygen and vacuum, imaging room shielding, electrical loads for equipment that wasn't fully specified at design time — each of these is a potential handoff failure. Each one is a change order waiting to happen.

## Change Orders Are Where Projects Die

The formal mechanism for the blame game is the change order. A change order is what happens when reality diverges from the plan — and with siloed architecture and construction, it diverges constantly. The architect drew something. The contractor found a condition in the field the drawing didn't account for. Now someone has to decide who pays to reconcile the two.

In a project where design and construction are separate entities with separate contracts and separate financial incentives, change orders become adversarial. The contractor is incentivized to issue them because they're often the only way to protect margin on a low-bid job. The architect is incentivized to blame field conditions. You are incentivized to avoid conflict, because you have a business to run, and every hour you spend on a construction dispute is an hour you're not seeing patients, treating animals, caring for kids, or managing your facility.

So you approve the change orders. And they stack up. And the project comes in over budget and late, and you're left holding a space that is no one's best work.

## What In-House Architecture Actually Means

There's a structural solution to this problem, and it's not better contracts or more aggressive project management on your end. It's having your architect and your contractor be the same organization, accountable to the same outcome.

When the people drawing your plans are the same people building your facility — when they sit in the same building, report to the same leadership, and get measured on the same result — the dynamic changes completely. There's no one to blame. There's no boundary to retreat behind. There's just a problem that needs to get solved, and a team that owns solving it.

It also means the design happens with full knowledge of how it will actually be built. Not idealized, not theoretical — real. Specs that account for where the vacuum line actually runs. Slab plans that incorporate floor drains for veterinary treatment areas before the concrete is poured. Electrical plans coordinated with equipment vendors before the walls are framed. Details that don't need to be re-interpreted in the field because the person interpreting them is standing next to the person who drew them.
