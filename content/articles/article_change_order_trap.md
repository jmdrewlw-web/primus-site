# The Low Bid That Wasn't Low

There is a contractor type that has been operating the same way for decades, and the construction industry has essentially normalized it to the point where owners who get burned by it often blame themselves. The pattern is straightforward: win the job with the lowest number, then rebuild margin through change orders once the contract is signed and work has started. By project completion, the "low" bid has become the most expensive one on the table. The owner is over budget, frequently behind schedule, and has no leverage because the work is already underway.

Understanding how this works mechanically is the first step to not falling for it.

The bid process creates an incentive problem. When multiple contractors submit proposals on the same project, the owner typically selects based heavily on price. The contractor who wins on price gets the project. The contractor who wins on price by being the most thorough estimator and genuinely finding efficiencies gets the project for good reasons. The contractor who wins on price by deliberately underbidding — leaving things out, making assumptions in their favor, pricing unknowns at zero — gets the project for bad ones. From the outside, at the moment of selection, those two contractors look identical. They don't start looking different until change orders begin.

A lean bid is often lean in specific, calculated ways. General conditions — the overhead costs of running the job like supervision, temporary facilities, and insurance — might be underestimated. Allowances for finishes, hardware, or equipment might be set below what the actual project requires. Work that a reasonable estimator would include in scope might be excluded based on a technically defensible reading of the drawings. Electrical panel capacity might be priced at existing conditions with no provision for the upgrade that any experienced estimator would know is likely. Structural work in renovation projects might assume favorable existing conditions with no contingency for what's actually in the walls. The estimate isn't fraudulent. Each omission has a justification. But the cumulative effect is a starting number that doesn't reflect the real cost of the project.

Work begins. The contractor mobilizes, the owner is committed, and then the change orders start arriving. They come one at a time, and each one feels reasonable when you read the justification. The electrical panel upgrade wasn't included in the base scope because the drawings didn't explicitly call for one — but now that the load calculations are complete, it's clearly necessary, and here's the change order for $18,000. The floor flatness specification in the dental operatories requires a level of slab preparation that wasn't included in the base bid because it wasn't in the specifications — and here's the change order for $12,000. The landlord's base building doesn't include the mechanical equipment originally assumed in the bid — and here's the change order for $27,000. The paint allowance was based on a standard commercial grade and the owner selected a product that's two price points higher — and here's the change order for $8,000.

None of these change orders are outrageous on their own. Each one is a real cost and a documented scope item. But add them up — and add to them the six or eight similar items that arrive over the course of a four-month build — and the low bid that came in at $1.1M has become a $1.4M project. The bid that was $100,000 higher and didn't win would have come in at $1.25M. The owner saved $100,000 on paper and spent $300,000 more in practice.

The timing makes it worse. Change orders don't all arrive at the beginning. They arrive throughout the project, often at moments when work is in progress and stopping is more expensive than approving. When the electrical panel change order arrives during rough-in, the practical alternative to approving it is stopping work and rebidding the project — which is not actually a realistic option. The contractor knows this. The leverage shifts the moment the contract is signed and the first crew shows up. What felt like a negotiation is now much less of one.

This is why the bid comparison exercise — putting three numbers side by side and picking the lowest — is one of the most misleading processes in commercial construction. You cannot compare bids that don't contain the same scope. And if one contractor submitted a thorough scope and another submitted a lean one, the numbers on paper are not comparable no matter how much they look like they are.

The only way to make a bid comparison meaningful is to start with a complete, well-documented scope and require every bidder to price exactly that scope. Complete architectural drawings, written specifications, equipment coordination included, all subcontractor scopes defined. When everyone prices the same project, the differences in the bids reflect actual differences in efficiency, overhead structure, and subcontractor relationships — not differences in what was included. That's a comparison you can make intelligently.

Fixed pricing from a contractor who produced the drawings themselves — or who participated fully in the design process — is the structure that closes this gap. When the design and construction team are integrated, the scope is defined before the price is set. The estimate is built from drawings that actually represent what the project requires, not drawings that were available to bid and left interpretation to the contractor. The price that comes out of that process is real because it was built on real information.

The cheapest bid is only cheap if it includes everything. The only way to know what it includes is to look carefully at what it doesn't.
