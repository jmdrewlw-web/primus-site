# The Most Underrated Part of a Construction Project: Knowing Who to Call

There's a version of a construction project that looks organized from the outside. You have an architect. You have a general contractor. You have an engineer of record. You have an equipment vendor, a furniture vendor, maybe a separate technology contractor for your IT and telecom rough-in. All of these people are professionals. All of them are doing their jobs. And yet somehow, you — the dentist, the veterinarian, the physician, the daycare operator — have become the general contractor of your own project, because nobody else has the whole picture.

This is how most commercial construction actually works, and most people don't realize it until they're in it. The architect finishes the drawings and considers their primary obligation largely complete. The GC is responsible for executing those drawings, not for identifying what's missing from them. The structural engineer is coordinating with the architect but not necessarily with the equipment vendor whose machine needs a reinforced slab. The equipment vendor is quoting lead times to you directly, but nobody is translating those lead times into the construction schedule. When the structural drawings come in late and push the steel delivery and compress the framing timeline, you find out about it because the GC called you. You are the communication hub. You are the one deciding what information goes to whom.

What that actually feels like in practice is a second job. You're fielding calls from three different parties on the same day about three problems that are all related but that none of them have connected. You're making decisions that should be handled by someone with construction expertise because nobody else has the authority to make them. You're the one who knows that a piece of critical equipment is on back-order because you talked to the vendor last week, but you forgot to tell the GC, and now there's a scheduling conflict nobody caught until it was too late to fix cheaply. Meanwhile you have a practice to run, patients to see, and a staff operating out of a temporary space asking you weekly when the move date is.

The reason "single point of contact" sounds like a marketing phrase is that it gets used as one. But the underlying problem it's solving is real and significant. When one entity owns the architecture and the construction — when the same firm is drawing the plans and building the building — the coordination that normally falls to you becomes internal. The architect doesn't hand off to the contractor. They are the contractor. When the structural drawings are late, the project manager who calls about it is talking to a colleague in the same organization, and the fix happens without you managing the conversation. When the equipment lead time is going to conflict with the construction schedule, someone catches it because they're responsible for both.

This is what clients remember after the project is over. Not the square footage. Not the finishes. They remember whether they were running the project or whether someone else was. When a practice goes from Thursday close to Monday open — that's a story about execution, about someone owning every detail of the handoff. The practices that saw patient numbers double or production jump after opening — part of that is the space, but part of it is that they got through construction without losing six months of their professional bandwidth managing contractor relationships.

The fragmentation model — separate architect, separate GC, owner in the middle — isn't inherently corrupt. Plenty of projects get built that way. But the risk in that model is carried by you. Every gap between the architect's drawing and the contractor's execution is a gap you're navigating. Every schedule conflict between vendors is a conflict you're resolving. If you have the time and the construction expertise to manage that, it can work. Most practice owners don't, and they find out mid-project when it's too late to change the structure.

What a genuine single point of contact actually gives you is the ability to focus on opening your practice instead of building it. Someone else is responsible for the whole outcome — not the drawings, not the construction, but the finished, functional, licensed, operational space. That accountability is worth more than most people price it until they've experienced what happens when nobody has it.
