# You Built It. Now What? The Grand Opening Strategy Nobody Plans Far Enough Ahead

Most operators spend 12 to 18 months thinking about construction. They track the site plan, review the floor plan revisions, walk the job site, debate finishes and fixtures, and count down the days to completion. Then they spend about three weeks thinking about what happens when the doors open.

The mismatch is a problem. The building is the platform. What fills it — patients, clients, referrals, community relationships — does not arrive automatically because you built something. It arrives because you planned for it, and planning takes longer than three weeks.

If you want a real opening, the planning starts at month six of construction. Here is what that looks like.

**Communicating the move to your existing patient base.**

If you are relocating from an existing location, you have a patient or client list. Those people need to know what is happening, when it is happening, where the new location is, and what changes — if anything — they should expect in how they interact with your practice. For a practice with 3,000 active patients, this is a real communication operation. A single postcard mailed at the 30-day mark is not enough. A well-executed transition communication includes a multi-touch sequence — early announcement via email and text at 90 days, a direct mail piece at 60 days, reminder communications in the 30-day window, confirmation of appointments with new address attached, and a final push in the week before opening. Every touchpoint should include the new address, new phone number if it changed, and clear instruction on what patients need to do.

What gets missed most often: operators assume their patient base will figure it out. Some will. Others will book with a competitor because they couldn't find you when they tried to schedule.

**Managing the transition logistics.**

Dr. Gleason closed on a Thursday and opened in his new location the following Monday. That kind of transition does not happen by accident. It requires a moving plan — equipment, records, supplies — that is mapped out weeks in advance. It requires that your scheduling software is reconfigured for the new space before the first patient walks in the door. It requires that your staff knows the new layout, knows where supplies are staged, knows the new workflows well enough to execute them without hesitation on day one.

The operators who struggle on opening week are almost always the ones who treated the operational setup as something to figure out in the last ten days. Run a pre-opening walkthrough with your team. Simulate patient flow through the new space. Make sure every exam room, every workstation, every supply closet is stocked and organized before you open. The building is ready. Your team needs to be ready too.

**The grand opening event itself.**

A grand opening is not a party. It is a relationship event. The people you invite matter: referring practitioners in adjacent specialties, neighboring businesses who share your parking lot or strip center, local professionals who interact with your patient population — attorneys, financial advisors, school administrators for pediatric practices, veterinarians for medical practices. The purpose is to build relationships that produce ongoing referrals and community connection, not to throw a celebration for people who already know you.

Timing matters as well. If you can open on a date that connects to a local event — a community fair, a neighborhood business association meeting — you extend your reach without additional cost. If you can get a local civic figure to attend, the photo opportunity and social media value is real.

**Marketing ramp in the first 90 days.**

New patient acquisition does not happen on its own in a new location. The 90 days after opening are the period where aggressive marketing has the highest return. Google Ads targeting your service area and vertical. Social content showing the new space — behind the scenes, team introductions, photos that communicate quality without being clinical. Community partnerships with neighboring businesses. Referral source cultivation for practitioners in adjacent specialties.

The operators who grow fastest after opening are the ones who treated the first 90 days as a launch campaign, not a soft open.

**Digital presence — updated before patients try to navigate there.**

This is the item that gets missed most consistently. Your Google Business profile needs to be updated to the new address before you open — not the week after. Your website needs to reflect the new location, new parking instructions, new photos of the space. Every directory where your practice name appears — Yelp, Healthgrades, Zocdoc, Apple Maps, Bing, everywhere — needs to be updated. If a patient searches for you on opening week and Google sends them to your old address, you have lost that patient.

Set a deadline: all digital presence updated two weeks before opening, minimum. Assign one person to own this task and verify it is complete.

**The staffing reality of the opening spike.**

Every well-marketed opening generates an inquiry spike in the first two to four weeks. More calls than usual. More appointment requests. More new patient inquiries than your team may have been handling in the prior location. If you are not staffed to handle the volume — if calls are going to voicemail, if new patient scheduling has a two-week wait — you are losing revenue that you spent months creating the conditions to generate.

Before you open, assess your front desk capacity honestly. If you added clinical space and you expect a patient volume increase, your administrative capacity needs to scale with it. The worst version of this problem is a practice that does a beautiful opening, generates real demand, and then loses that demand because the phone isn't being answered.
