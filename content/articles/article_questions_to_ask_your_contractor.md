# The Questions That Separate Experienced Contractors From the Ones Learning on Your Project

There is a version of the contractor vetting process that feels like due diligence but is mostly theater. You ask for a portfolio. They show you photographs. You ask for references. They give you three names who are almost certainly going to say nice things. You shake hands, sign a contract, and find out eighteen months later that your project manager had never overseen a dental build before, that your contractor's subcontractors had never worked in a medical environment, and that the problems you are now paying to fix were entirely predictable by anyone who had done this before.

The photographs were real. The references were real. The contractor was not lying to you. They were just not experienced in the specific kind of project you hired them to build — and the questions you asked didn't surface that distinction. The right questions are not designed to catch a contractor in a dishonest moment. They are designed to start a conversation that reveals, through the quality and specificity of the answers, whether this person has actually done this before.

The first and most important question is simply: how many projects of this type have you completed, and can I speak with operators from those projects? Not "have you built medical or dental facilities" — that gets you a yes from almost anyone with a commercial portfolio. The question is how many, and specifically what kind. A contractor who has built twelve dental offices in the last five years speaks a different language than one who built a general medical office park and is now positioning that as healthcare experience. They understand operatory layouts, the infrastructure requirements for dental equipment, the coordination required between mechanical, electrical, and plumbing trades to make chair delivery functional. They have made the mistakes already — on other people's projects — and they know how to avoid them on yours. When you ask this question and then ask for references from those specific projects, you will immediately learn something. A contractor with genuine depth in your building type will have no hesitation giving you a list of ten clients. A contractor who is stretching their portfolio will give you a short list with caveats. That response tells you everything.

The second question is about their current backlog and their genuine availability to start your project. This is where many operators get misled, not because the contractor is being dishonest, but because optimism is structurally built into how contractors talk about their schedules. A contractor who is finishing two projects and has three more in the pipeline may sincerely believe they can start your project in April. They may be wrong. When you ask what their current project load looks like, what will be completed before your project begins, and what their project management capacity actually is — meaning how many active projects one project manager is currently overseeing — you get a much clearer picture of whether your project will receive real attention or whether it will be managed as one of several concurrent obligations. The answer to "what does your project manager's current workload look like" is one of the most informative things a contractor can tell you, and it is a question most operators never think to ask.

Subcontractor depth in your specific market is the third area worth exploring in real detail. A contractor's reputation is partly a product of their own organization and partly a product of the tradespeople they work with consistently. In a market where they have done ten projects, they have relationships with electricians, plumbers, and mechanical contractors who know their standards, who show up when they say they will, and who have worked out the coordination problems that always exist between trades on a complex build. In a market where they are doing their first project, they are assembling those relationships for the first time — and you are the project on which those relationships are being tested. Ask them directly: who are your primary subcontractors in this market, and how long have you worked with them? A confident, specific answer with names and project histories tells you something very different than a vague answer about how they source trades locally.

Change orders are worth a full conversation, not just a question. Change orders are where projects get expensive in ways that feel ambiguous — where operators feel like something is off but can't quite articulate whether they're being taken advantage of or simply paying for real scope changes. The question to ask is not whether you will have change orders. You will. The question is how they price them, what their process is for surfacing a potential change before it becomes a cost, and whether there is a documented approval process before any work outside original scope proceeds. A contractor who describes a clear, documented process — where any potential change is flagged before it is executed, where pricing is provided before authorization, where the client has a genuine opportunity to decide — is describing a professional operation. A contractor who describes change orders in vague terms, or who implies that things sometimes just need to happen and get sorted out later, is describing a process that produces unpleasant surprises. Ask for a copy of their standard change order documentation. If they have a mature process, this is not an unusual request.

Communication during the project is the last area to explore, and it is the one that most directly affects your experience of the build itself. Ask specifically: who is your primary point of contact for the duration of the project, will that person change at any point, and what does your reporting cadence look like? How will you communicate schedule status, and what is your process when the project is running behind? The answer you are looking for is a single accountable person, a defined rhythm of communication that does not require you to chase information, and a candid acknowledgment that they will tell you bad news promptly rather than optimistically. A contractor who tells you they have an in-house project coordinator, that you will have a direct line to that person throughout the build, and that they send weekly written updates is describing something very different from a contractor who implies you can always call someone if you have a question.

None of these questions are designed to be adversarial. The best contractors in any market welcome them, because they have good answers. They have built your building type before. Their schedule is honest. Their subcontractors are proven. Their change order process is documented. Their communication is proactive. When you sit across from a contractor who answers all of these questions with confidence and specificity, you are sitting across from someone who has done this before — and that is the only contractor you want building your facility.
