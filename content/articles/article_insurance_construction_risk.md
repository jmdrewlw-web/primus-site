# The Construction Insurance Gaps That Can Cost You Everything

Most owners who sign a construction contract spend more time reviewing the payment schedule than the insurance requirements. The payment schedule matters — but the insurance requirements determine what happens when something goes wrong, and something almost always goes wrong. The question is whether it costs you the price of a deductible or the price of a lawsuit.

Construction insurance is not one thing. It is a collection of policies, from multiple parties, covering different risks, with different limits and different exclusions. Understanding what you have, what your contractor has, and where the gaps are is not something most owners think to do until they are staring at an uninsured loss.

**Builder's risk**

Builder's risk insurance covers property damage to the project during construction — theft of materials stored on-site, fire damage to a partially framed building, weather events that damage work in progress. It is essentially property insurance for a building that does not yet exist as a completed structure.

What it does not cover is equally important. Builder's risk policies typically exclude damage caused by design errors or faulty workmanship — if a roof leaks because it was designed incorrectly, builder's risk does not respond. They exclude consequential losses from delay — if a fire sets the project back four months and your lease on your current space runs out in the meantime, the cost of that bridge lease is not covered. They exclude contractor financial failure — if your contractor goes bankrupt mid-project, builder's risk does not pay to complete the work.

Who pays for the builder's risk policy depends on what your contract says. On some projects it is the owner's obligation; on others the contractor carries it. Either way, verify that it is in place before construction starts and review the policy for the exclusions that matter most for your specific project.

**Workers' compensation and subcontractor coverage**

If a worker is injured on your construction site, your primary protection is the contractor's workers' compensation policy. Workers' comp is the exclusive remedy in most states — a covered worker cannot sue you personally if they have a valid workers' comp claim against their employer.

The gap opens when a subcontractor does not carry workers' comp — either because they let their policy lapse, because they misclassified workers as independent contractors, or because the GC hired uninsured subs to cut costs. An uninsured worker injured on your site may have the ability to pursue a claim directly against you as the property owner. In some states, you may have statutory liability as the downstream employer when the immediate employer has no insurance.

The protection is straightforward in principle: require your GC to provide certificates of insurance for every subcontractor before they step foot on-site. In practice, this requires follow-through — certificates need to be current, policy dates need to be verified, and a sub who starts work before the certificate arrives needs to stop until it does.

**General liability — limits and completed operations**

The contractor's general liability policy covers bodily injury and property damage caused by their operations. The limits on this policy should be proportionate to your project size. A contractor carrying $1 million per occurrence limits on a $3 million project has a coverage gap if a serious incident exceeds that limit.

Completed operations coverage is the provision within a general liability policy that extends protection after the contractor's work is finished. If a construction defect causes a bodily injury or property damage claim two years after substantial completion, completed operations coverage is what the contractor's policy uses to respond. Many contractors carry this coverage, but it is time-limited — typically expiring after one to three years — and some policies exclude it on certain project types.

**The scenario that keeps owners up at night**

The situation that most dramatically illustrates completed operations risk goes like this: a project is completed, the contractor is paid in full, and eighteen months later water intrusion begins damaging finished space and equipment. Investigation reveals a flashing detail installed incorrectly during construction. The GC's general liability policy has been renewed since project completion; the new policy has a prior work exclusion. The completed operations coverage under the original policy lapsed. The contractor disputes liability. You are pursuing remedies against a contractor with no applicable insurance coverage, which means you are pursuing remedies against a contractor who will tell you to sue them.

The litigation cost, the remediation cost, and the business interruption cost are yours to absorb while the legal process runs its course.

**Three questions to ask before signing**

These are not difficult questions, but they require asking before you are under contract, when you still have leverage.

First: does the contractor's general liability policy include completed operations coverage, and what are its limits and duration? Ask for the declarations page of the policy, not just the certificate.

Second: who is responsible for builder's risk, and is the policy in place before any work starts? Verify the answer rather than assuming it.

Third: does the contractor require certificates of insurance from all subcontractors before they access the site, and will they provide you copies? The procedure for enforcing this should be in the contract, not just in conversation.

Adding yourself as an additional insured on the contractor's general liability policy is standard practice and should be a non-negotiable term in your contract. As an additional insured, the contractor's policy can respond directly to a claim brought against you arising from their operations, rather than leaving you to seek indemnification through litigation after the fact.

Construction insurance is not a detail. It is the financial structure that determines what you are actually buying when you sign a contract. The projects that end well are not necessarily the ones where nothing went wrong. They are the ones where the coverage was in place when it needed to be.
