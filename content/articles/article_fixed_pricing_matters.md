# Fixed Price vs. Cost-Plus: The Construction Contract That Can Break You

There are two fundamental ways to structure a construction contract. In a fixed-price agreement, the contractor commits to a defined scope of work for a defined number. If costs come in higher than anticipated, the contractor absorbs the difference. In a cost-plus agreement, the contractor is reimbursed for actual costs — materials, labor, subcontractor invoices — plus a predetermined fee or percentage on top. The owner pays whatever the project actually costs.

On paper, cost-plus sounds transparent. You see every invoice. There's no markup buried in a lump sum. But transparency in billing is not the same as cost control. Under cost-plus, the budget risk sits entirely with the owner. The contractor has very little financial incentive to find the most efficient solution, push back on subcontractor pricing, or manage scope creep aggressively. Every dollar that gets added to the project is a dollar the contractor is reimbursed for, plus their percentage on top. The contractor isn't dishonest — the structure just doesn't align incentives the way owners assume it does.

For commercial builds in general, this dynamic is manageable if the scope is tightly defined and conditions are predictable. But for medical, dental, and clinical facilities, cost-plus is a genuine risk exposure that owners frequently underestimate.

Consider a realistic scenario. A dental practice owner undertakes a $2M cost-plus build. The project scope is documented but not exhaustive — design decisions are still being made when construction starts, which is common when there's no complete drawing set prior to breaking ground. During construction, the owner decides to add two operatories beyond the original count. The equipment vendor changes the sterilization center configuration, which requires repositioning plumbing rough-ins. The owner selects higher-end millwork than was in the allowance. Materials that were estimated at one price have escalated by the time they're ordered. The electrical panel, which was assumed to have adequate capacity, requires an upgrade once the full equipment load is calculated. None of these are the contractor's fault. Every change is documented and approved.

By project completion, that $2M build is at $2.7M. The contractor is not overcharging. They invoiced what it cost plus their fee. The owner simply absorbed every unknown condition, every scope change, and every market fluctuation — because that is how cost-plus works. There was no mechanism to protect the budget because the contract didn't require the contractor to protect it.

Fixed pricing works differently. The contractor takes responsibility for a thoroughly defined scope. If the estimate was wrong, if a subcontractor comes in higher than expected, if materials cost more than anticipated — that's the contractor's problem to solve. The discipline required to offer a genuine fixed price is significant. You cannot commit to a number without complete drawings. You cannot commit to a number without coordinating equipment specifications. You cannot commit to a number without identifying potential unknown conditions and deciding how to handle them contractually. The act of producing a fixed-price proposal forces a level of scope definition that protects the owner whether or not they fully appreciate why.

This is why fixed pricing and complete drawings are inseparable. You'll sometimes hear a contractor offer a "fixed price" based on a schematic design or a preliminary scope document. That is not fixed pricing — that is a placeholder number with a scope broad enough to justify nearly any change order that follows. A legitimate fixed price comes after the drawings are complete, the specifications are written, equipment is selected and coordinated, and every subcontractor scope is defined. The number that comes out of that process is one the contractor can stand behind, because they've actually thought through what the project requires.

The practical implication for owners is this: if a contractor is willing to give you a price without complete drawings, that price is not fixed. It's an estimate dressed in fixed-price language that will grow as the scope becomes real. The change orders won't feel arbitrary. Each one will be technically justified. But the cumulative result is a project that started at one number and ended at a very different one.

Fixed-price contracting also changes the pre-construction phase in a way that benefits owners. Because the contractor has to get to a firm number, they do the coordination work that would otherwise happen during construction as expensive surprises. Equipment specifications get confirmed before permits are submitted. Structural systems get designed around actual mechanical and electrical loads. Materials with long lead times get identified and ordered. The project is built on paper before it's built in the field, which means the field work proceeds with far fewer interruptions.

The comparison most owners make — lowest bid from contractor A versus higher bid from contractor B — is often not a comparison of the same thing. If contractor A's bid is cost-plus or based on incomplete scope, and contractor B's bid is fixed-price from a complete drawing set, those numbers don't mean the same thing. The only bid you can genuinely compare is a fixed-price bid from a contractor who has actually defined what the scope contains. Everything else is a starting point with unknowns still embedded in it.
