# What "In-House Architecture" Actually Means and Why It Changes Everything

The phrase "design-build" gets used by a lot of firms. So does "in-house architecture." But those two things can mean very different realities depending on who's saying them. Most firms that market themselves as design-build are, in practice, general contractors who maintain a relationship with one or two outside architectural firms they bring in regularly. That's not in-house. That's a referral arrangement with a handshake on top.

True in-house architecture means the architects are employees of the same company as the builders. They sit in the same building. They use the same drawing sets. They go to the same project meetings. When something in the field doesn't match what was specified, the person who specified it is a phone call away — not an external vendor navigating their own contract obligations, billing structure, and liability exposure.

That distinction is not trivial. It is the difference between a project that runs smoothly and one that runs into the oldest, most expensive problem in construction: the gap between design intent and field execution.

Here's how that gap forms under the conventional model. An owner hires an architect. The architect produces drawings. Those drawings go out to bid. A general contractor is selected. The GC takes the drawings and starts building. During construction — always during construction — the GC's team finds things that don't quite work. A structural element conflicts with a mechanical run. The equipment layout specified doesn't match the rough-in dimensions the equipment vendor actually ships. A detail calls for a material that's no longer available or has a 14-week lead time no one priced. These are coordination problems. They happen on almost every project.

Under the conventional model, those problems land in a three-party dispute. The GC says the drawings were wrong. The architect says the GC should have flagged it sooner. The owner is in the middle, watching the conversation and wondering who's going to pay for the fix. The fix itself gets documented as a change order. The change order costs money. It also costs time, because work can't proceed until the change is approved and the revised scope is priced. A single change order during structural framing can pause a project for a week. A dozen of them over the course of a build can push a completion date by months.

When the architect and builder are on the same team, working under the same organizational accountability, that dynamic fundamentally changes. Coordination problems don't become disputes — they become conversations. The architect who produced the drawing and the superintendent trying to execute it can talk to each other without anyone's contract getting involved. The problem gets resolved at a desk, before work stops, before concrete gets poured in the wrong place, before someone has to write a change order.

This matters even more in specialized facility types — dental offices, medical clinics, veterinary practices, daycare centers. These are not generic commercial interiors. They have equipment requirements that have to be integrated into the building structure from the beginning. A dental office has chair placements, delivery systems, plumbing requirements, overhead cabinetry, and sterilization centers that all have to align. If the equipment specifications aren't in the drawings before permits are pulled, those specifications are going to surface later — as changes. The more complex the equipment coordination, the more critical it is that the architect and builder are working from the same information at the same time.

Permitting is another place where the gap matters. Local jurisdictions have specific requirements for healthcare facilities, food service, childcare, and industrial uses. An external architect who isn't familiar with a given county's health department review process, or who doesn't have a working relationship with the local building department, produces drawings that may not sail through review. The in-house team that has built in a jurisdiction before knows what reviewers ask for. They can structure the submission to anticipate common comments. That translates directly into faster permit issuance and fewer revision cycles — weeks saved before a shovel touches dirt.

There is also the question of accountability. When architecture and construction are separate contracts, each party has legal obligations to their own interests and liability exposure to manage. That creates a system where the architect has to document everything to protect themselves, and the GC has to document everything to protect themselves, and the owner ends up managing correspondence between two entities who are each watching their own backs. A unified team — one contract, one point of accountability — means disputes are internal. The entity responsible for the project is responsible for the whole project, not just their slice of it.

For owners who've built before under a traditional model, the contrast is immediately apparent. The phone calls you used to make to the architect — "the GC says this needs to change, is that right?" — don't exist. The meetings where the architect and GC argue about whose problem a coordination issue is — they don't happen. The change order review process, where you're trying to decide whether you're being charged fairly for something you don't fully understand — compressed significantly, because the scope was coordinated before it left the drawing set.
