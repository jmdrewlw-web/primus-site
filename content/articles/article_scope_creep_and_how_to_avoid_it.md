# Scope Creep: How Projects Expand and How to Keep Yours from Getting Away from You

Every construction project that goes significantly over budget has a story. And in almost every one of those stories, the overrun didn't happen because of a single catastrophic event. It happened because of accumulated additions, each one of which seemed reasonable at the time. A different finish here. An extra electrical circuit there. One more exam room reconfigured. A different tile in the entry than what was originally specified. Scope creep is the tax on indecision and impulse, and it compounds the way interest compounds — slowly at first, then all at once, and always at the worst possible moment.

Understanding scope creep starts with recognizing that it comes from two distinct directions, and that each requires a different defense.

The first kind is operator-initiated scope creep. This is the most common and, paradoxically, the hardest to defend against, because the impulse comes from you. You walk the job site at week six and realize the break room you designed doesn't have enough counter space. You see the casework going in and decide you want a different pull than what you specified. Your spouse visits the space and thinks the reception area should have a feature wall. Your practice consultant suggests adding a consultation room you didn't originally plan for. Every one of these changes feels small in isolation. None of them is small in practice. Mid-construction additions and modifications cost three to five times what they would have cost if they'd been part of the original scope, and that multiplier is not an exaggeration. When you add scope in the middle of a project, you're paying for the work itself, plus the disruption to work that's already in progress or completed, plus the coordination time to integrate the change, plus whatever design or engineering rework is required. The physical cost of a different light fixture is trivial. The cost of pulling that fixture from a ceiling that's been dramed, rerouting conduit, repatching drywall, and repainting is not.

The discipline required to prevent operator-initiated scope creep is uncomfortable and requires genuine self-awareness. It means being honest about the difference between something you actually need and something that feels appealing in the moment because you're spending money anyway and it seems like a small increment on a large number. It means trusting the planning process — specifically, trusting that if you did the design phase right, the decisions you made then were sound decisions, and the impulse to change them mid-construction is more likely to be situational than strategic. It means building a pre-construction phase long enough and rigorous enough that the design is genuinely locked before ground breaks, so that you're not discovering things you wish you'd decided differently while tradespeople are already on site.

The second kind of scope creep is contractor-initiated, and it operates through a different mechanism. This isn't always bad-faith behavior, but it can be. Contractor-initiated scope expansion typically shows up as change orders — formal requests to authorize additional work and cost that the contractor claims falls outside the original contract scope. Some change orders are legitimate. Site conditions that were genuinely unknowable before excavation, code interpretations that surface during permitting, owner-requested changes — these represent real scope that wasn't in the original contract. But change orders can also be a revenue mechanism for contractors who bid projects low to win the job and plan to recover margin through approved changes during construction. The operator who doesn't understand what they signed — what's actually in scope and what's not — is vulnerable to this.

Fixed-price contracts are the primary protection against contractor-initiated scope games. A contract that specifies a firm price for a clearly defined scope of work puts the burden of cost overruns on the contractor, not on you. If the scope is clear and the price is fixed, the contractor cannot come back to you three months in and claim that something basic to the project wasn't included without a legitimate basis. This is why detailed design documents and specifications matter enormously — not because they're an administrative formality, but because they define the boundaries of the fixed-price contract. The more ambiguous the drawings and specs, the more room a contractor has to claim that what you want is different from what was included. The less ambiguous they are, the less traction a spurious change order has.

The locked scope is not a constraint on a good project. It is what a good project requires. Dr. Revell at Des Moines Children's Dentistry in Iowa found that competitors were willing to pay two times more for less space than what Primus delivered — outcomes like that are made possible by pre-construction discipline that locks scope before work begins and holds that scope through completion. The operators who resist the temptation to add, modify, and improve their way through a live construction project end up with buildings that cost what they were supposed to cost, open when they were supposed to open, and deliver the value that was planned. That's not because they made no good decisions during construction — it's because the good decisions were made earlier, when making them was cheap.

There is a practical exercise that helps. Before authorizing any change order or modification during construction, ask yourself what this same change would have cost at the design table before construction started. Then multiply that number by four. If the modified cost still makes sense, the change may be worth making. If it doesn't, you have your answer. The discipline of the locked scope isn't rigidity — it's honesty about where good decisions are made cheaply versus where they're made expensively. Cheap is at the design table. Expensive is on the job site.
